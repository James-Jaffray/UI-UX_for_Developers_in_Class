# UI-UX for Developers — Day 17: Low-Fidelity Wireframes

**Today's focus:** what wireframes and prototypes are, why start low-fidelity, and how to sketch one. Comes after [[User Flows and Information Architecture]] (the flow tells you which screens you need). The demos' `wireframe.png` files are what you've been building from in CSS.

## Wireframe vs Prototype
| | Wireframe | Prototype |
|---|---|---|
| What | static, low-fidelity **structure** of screens | interactive **simulation** of the end product |
| Shows | layout, navigation, where text and graphics go | flow and behaviour |

Both pin down structure, flow and function **before any code**. Wireframes are usually monochrome: simple shapes and lines marking navigation, text and graphics. Research cited in class: they can cut design time by up to 50%.

## Levels of Fidelity ("Detail")
Detail = how close it is to the final product, judged by layout accuracy, content completeness, and interactivity.

| Level | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Low-fi** | quick rough sketches of structure and core functionality |
| **Mid-fi** | more visual and functional detail |
| **High-fi** | detailed, interactive mockups close to the real product (also called prototypes) |

## Low-Fi Wireframes
Made early, after brainstorming and concept work. Basic shapes, lines and placeholders; no images or polished text. Done on paper or in minutes in Balsamiq, InVision or Figma.

**Benefits**
- Feedback on **function and usability**, not aesthetics
- Fast changes (agile)
- Catch problems early, saving time and money
- One shared picture for designers, developers and stakeholders
- Client approval before detailed work; becomes a roadmap
- Keeps focus on user scenarios

**Disadvantages**
- Hard to picture the real experience
- Not visually appealing to stakeholders
- No interactivity, so user scenarios can't be tested
- Can be misread by people unfamiliar with wireframes
- Less useful later; high-fi needed as the project moves on

**Use them** early, after research and ideation, before any visual styling, and when exploring user journeys or task flows.

## Kinds of Examples
- **Hand-drawn basic:** 3–4 main sections, no text, no colour
- **Hand-drawn advanced:** named sections (e.g. Popular, Search, Your Tickets, Profile, Friends) with buttons, nav bars and content areas outlined
- **Responsive:** shows how the layout adapts to desktop, tablet and mobile. Best practice is **mobile first**
- **Mobile app:** UI elements as basic geometric shapes

## Best Practices
- Avoid pixel perfection
- Annotate actions
- Include only essential elements
- Highlight clickable areas
- Get feedback early and often

## Tools
Paper + pen (fastest iteration), Balsamiq (digital sketch style), Figma / InVision (free, collaborative), Adobe XD (turn wireframes into prototypes).

## How to Sketch One
1. Grab pen and paper
2. Outline the layout with squares, rectangles, circles and lines
3. Add placeholders: text labels and simple shapes for images
4. Show it to users and stakeholders, refine, repeat

## To Know
- [ ] Wireframe = static structure; prototype = interactive simulation
- [ ] Low → mid → high fidelity; start low
- [ ] Low-fi pros: fast, cheap, focuses on function. Cons: static, hard to visualize, easy to misread
- [ ] Mobile first for responsive wireframes
- [ ] Annotate, skip pixel perfection, get feedback early

## Homework
- Create a low-fi prototype for a common UX problem (e.g. a checkout page)
- Practice: hand-drawn wireframe for a basic app
